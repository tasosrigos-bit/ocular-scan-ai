## Overview
Diabetic retinopathy is damage to the blood vessels of the retina caused by diabetes, and is the most common cause of vision loss in people with diabetes. High blood sugar over time damages the small blood vessels supplying the retina, causing them to leak fluid or bleed. The eye compensates by growing new, poorly-formed blood vessels, which can leak or bleed more easily — this progression characterizes the disease's advancing stages.

## Symptoms
Early-stage disease is typically asymptomatic. As it progresses, patients may notice fluctuating vision changes, and in later stages, dark floating spots or streaks from bleeding into the vitreous. These spots can sometimes clear on their own, but this does not mean the underlying disease has resolved.

## Risk Factors
Anyone with type 1, type 2, or gestational diabetes can develop DR. Risk increases the longer a person has had diabetes — more than half of people with diabetes eventually develop some degree of retinopathy. Pregnant women with diabetes are at particularly high risk.

## Management
Early-stage disease is typically monitored via regular dilated eye exams — sometimes as frequently as every 2–4 months once retinopathy is present. Blood sugar, blood pressure, and cholesterol control are important preventive measures. In later stages, active treatment includes anti-VEGF injections, corticosteroids, laser treatment, and — for significant bleeding or scarring — vitrectomy surgery. DR can also lead to DME, neovascular glaucoma, and tractional retinal detachment.

## Referral
Given that early-stage DR is asymptomatic but progressive, and later stages carry meaningful risk of permanent vision loss if untreated, any DR finding warrants ophthalmologic follow-up rather than being dismissed as incidental.
